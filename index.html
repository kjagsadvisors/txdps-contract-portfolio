How TXDPS would use this

Concrete steps a procurement officer takes with this dashboard, this week. Each step is a recurring workflow, not a one-time analysis.

Step 1 — Daily

Open the renewal queue.

Sort by days-to-expiry. The top of the queue is your action list for the day. Each row is a contract reaching its decision point: renew as-is, restructure, recompete, or sunset. Today: 318 contracts ($534M) need this review in the next 12 months.

Step 2 — Weekly

Triage the 16 patterns.

5 minutes per pattern. The contract manager closest to each pattern decides: deliberate (no action), worth investigating (route to category owner), or candidate for restructuring (escalate to procurement leadership). The dashboard records the decision so it doesn’t resurface.

Step 3 — Monthly

Review the lease portfolio.

41 leases signed before 2018 are still active. Each one was signed against an operational footprint that may or may not still apply. Validate against current facilities map. Identify the ones for which the building closed but the lease continues.

Step 4 — Quarterly

Refresh from updated source data.

Pull the updated TXDPS public contracts list (refreshed by DPS quarterly) and any internal CAPPS / contract management system data. The dashboard rebuilds with new patterns surfaced. Old decisions persist.

Patterns for stakeholder review

These are questions for the Department’s contract managers, not recommendations. Each card is a 5-minute review item. The contract manager confirms the pattern is deliberate or escalates for investigation.

A. Same-vendor cohort patterns

Multiple contracts with the same vendor whose descriptions follow a recognizable cohort pattern (e.g., A25, B25, C25, A26 — quarterly or class-based deployment cohorts). Could be deliberate (e.g., class-by-class equipment deployment) or an artifact of fiscal-year procurement. The contract owner knows which.

B. Same-product, multiple-vendor patterns

Contracts with very similar descriptions awarded to different vendors. Could be deliberate (multi-source for redundancy or geographic coverage) or an unintentional duplication. The category owner knows which.

C. Open-ended “blanket” contracts — 30 contracts, $168M

Contracts whose scope is open-ended (described as “blanket”, “as needed”, “T&M”, or similar) authorize draws against a fixed ceiling without per-purchase review. They concentrate decision authority and reduce visibility into actual utilization. Top 10 by ceiling shown.

D. Counterparty concentration — the “DPS [City] 2000 LLC” lease pattern

Sixteen single-purpose LLCs named “DPS [City] 2000 LLC” hold $407M in TXDPS facility leases. Public Texas SOS records show DPS Carrollton 2000 LLC has registered agent Jody L. Hagemann — an Austin real estate attorney. The naming pattern and shared registered agent strongly suggest common ownership across all 16 entities through one developer or real-estate fund. This pattern is NOT inherently shady. Build-to-suit single-purpose LLCs are completely standard in Texas commercial real estate. The procurement-relevant finding is counterparty concentration: $407M of long-term lease commitment likely concentrated with one beneficial owner. That creates renegotiation risk if the landlord’s financial position changes — and a portfolio-level negotiation lever TXDPS may not be using.

E. Same-vendor, same-day multi-contract patterns

Multiple contracts with the same vendor sharing the same begin date can indicate (a) deliberate splitting of one large purchase to stay under bid thresholds, (b) FY-aligned cohort splits for legitimate reasons, or (c) coincidental simultaneous solicitations. Worth a contract-manager check on the highest-value patterns.

F. Specific noteworthy contracts

Five contracts the system flags individually for size, term length, vendor entity oddities, or data-hygiene reasons. Each is a question, not an accusation.

What we cannot see from public data alone

The honest limits of this analysis — and what would change with TXDPS internal data access.

Limits of public-only analysis

  • Actual spend vs. contract ceiling. The TXDPS public PDF lists contract maximums. Actual payments per vendor would require Texas Comptroller payee payment records (cross-referenced via data.texas.gov). Contracts at high ceilings but low utilization are a separate optimization category we cannot surface here.
  • Programmatic context for each contract. Why a contract was structured the way it was, what program it supports, whether it’s tied to a federal grant with restrictions, whether the cohort split is intentional — only the contract owner knows.
  • Performance and deliverable acceptance status. Is the vendor delivering? Are SLAs being met? Are there outstanding issues? Internal contract management records hold this, not the public disclosure.
  • DIR cooperative status per contract. Whether each IT contract was procured through a DIR cooperative vehicle (DBITS, ITSAC, or IT Commodities) or directly — visible in DIR records but not in the §2054.126 disclosure.
  • Renewal options and price escalators. Many contracts have renewal options, price escalation clauses, or termination rights that change the actual decision space at renewal.
  • The reason a contract was signed. Sometimes contracts exist because of a specific event, audit finding, or stakeholder request. The institutional memory lives with the contract owner.

With internal data access — CAPPS / contract management system records, payment history from CAPPS, programmatic context from contract owners — the same dashboard methodology produces substantially deeper analysis. Underutilization gets surfaced. Performance issues get flagged. Contract-vs-program alignment gets validated. The system continues to operate the same way; the data quality improves.
